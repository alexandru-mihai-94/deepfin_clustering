Initial-value problems · one-step methods

Averaging the slopes

Every method here answers the same question — given the solution’s height now, where is it one step h from now? — and every one answers it by sampling the slope field f(t, y) a handful of times and taking a weighted average. Forward Euler samples once. Everything that beats it samples more, and averages better.

What order p buys you

Global error behaves like E ≈ Chp. Halve the step and Euler’s error halves; midpoint and Heun cut theirs to a quarter; RK4 cuts its to a sixteenth. That exponent, not the constant C, is what makes the difference worth four slope evaluations.

Why averaging works

Expand the exact step as a Taylor series. Euler keeps the hy′ term and drops ½h2y″. Averaging the slope at both ends of the step reproduces that second term too, so it cancels instead of accumulating — the trapezoid rule, applied to a curve you have not solved yet.

The floor at 10−16

On the log–log plot RK4’s line stops falling and turns ragged near 10−15. That is double-precision rounding, not the method giving up: once the truncation error drops below the noise in the arithmetic, smaller steps only add more roundoff.

How the step size is chosen

Adaptive mode takes each step twice — once at h, once as two halves — and reads the local error off the difference. Too big, and the step is rejected and retried smaller; comfortably small, and the next step stretches. Real solvers (RKF45, Dormand–Prince) get the same estimate from an embedded pair for almost nothing; step doubling is the version that works for any tableau, and it costs about three steps’ work per attempt.

Where “exact” comes from

The five built-in problems have closed-form solutions, so the error columns are exact. Type your own right-hand side and there is usually no formula to compare against — so the page integrates a reference solution with RK4 on 4000 steps and measures against that. It is good to roughly 10−12; below that you are reading the reference’s own error.

Accuracy is not stability

On y′ = −6y, Euler needs h < 2/6 just to stay bounded — past that its answer oscillates and grows while the true solution decays to nothing. Midpoint and Heun share that limit; RK4 holds on to h ≈ 0.46. Try it.
