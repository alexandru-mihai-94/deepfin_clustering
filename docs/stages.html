A clownfish in the making: spot the stage

These are the developmental stages of the clownfish Amphiprion ocellaris. As the babies grow they change shape and colour, and biologists sort them into seven stages (Roux et al., 2019), from a newly hatched, see-through larva (stage 1) to a juvenile with white bars (stage 7). Each mystery fish is somewhere in between. Can you tell which stage it is in?

How do biologists decide the stage?

The seven stages were defined by Roux and colleagues in Staging and normal table of postembryonic development of the clownfish (Amphiprion ocellaris), Developmental Dynamics (2019). Each fish is measured, and checked for features that appear as it grows:

Measurements
Standard lengthSnout to the tip of the notochord in young larvae; snout to the middle of the tail base once the tail has formed
Snout–anus lengthSnout to the anus
Head lengthSnout to the edge of the gill cover
Body depthFrom the top of the head, where the dorsal fin starts, straight down to where the pelvic fin attaches
Eye diameterAcross the longest axis of the eye
Snout lengthSnout to the front edge of the eye
Head depthAt right angles to the head length, from the anus to the base of the dorsal fin's soft rays
Features that appear
Anal finWhen its soft rays and its spines appear
Dorsal finWhen its soft rays and its spines appear
Pelvic finHow far it has developed
White stripesWhen each of the three white body stripes appears
Notochord flexionHow far the end of the notochord (the early backbone) has bent upward to form the tail

This is what DEEPFIN does. Without being shown a single labelled example, it sorted photos of young A. ocellaris into their developmental stages (as defined by Roux et al., 2019), getting about 87% of them right in our study. Instead of staging every fish by hand, a scientist only needs to check each group and fix the few strays.

Groups above use the experts' stage labels, to show what a finished sort looks like.

How does DEEPFIN do it?

A neural network that learned to see from over a million everyday photos (none of them baby clownfish) turns each picture into hundreds of numbers (512 to 1,280, depending on the network) describing its shapes, textures and colours. A method called UMAP then squeezes those numbers onto a flat map, so similar-looking fish land close together. Finally, a clustering step draws the boundaries between groups.
