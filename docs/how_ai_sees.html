How does an AI see a fish?

Build a classifier without training data

Computers don't see pictures. They see numbers. Follow one clownfish photo through a real neural network that has never seen these two species, and find out how it can still tell them apart, for example by checking… how much each fish looks like a bicycle?

Pick a fish

Two lab photos of each species, and one wild photo of each from iNaturalist.

Into the network

To a computer your photo is a grid of 224 × 224 dots, each with three colour numbers: 150,528 numbers. The network (MobileNetV2) passes them through dozens of layers. Each layer looks for patterns in the layer before. Here are a few of the patterns it found in your fish, from bright (strong) to dark (absent):

Early layers see small details on a fine grid. Deeper layers see bigger patterns on a coarser grid, until the whole fish is summed up in 1,280 numbers, and then scored against 1,000 everyday things.

The network's answer

The network learned from over a million photos of 1,000 everyday things: dogs, cars, pizza… and “anemone fish”, which is another name for clownfish.

But it has never heard of Ocellaris or Percula.

Both wild fish get the same answer:

A step back from the answer: the fingerprint

The final answer is no help: both species are just “anemone fish”. But just before it answers, the network sums up the photo in 1,280 numbers, a kind of fingerprint. It never learned our two species, yet fingerprints of the same species look alike.

Each thin stripe is one number, from low (dark) to high (bright). We lined the numbers up so the ones that differ most between the two species sit at opposite ends.

This is what DEEPFIN uses. It puts the fingerprints of all the fish on a map without being told which species is which, and the two species land in separate groups.

OcellarisPercula outlined: your fish

Each photo sits where DEEPFIN placed it, using only the 1,280-number fingerprints (MobileNetV2). The coloured borders show the species, which we added afterwards to check its work.

Your turn: build a classifier, no training needed

The network also gives every photo a score for each of the 1,000 things it knows: how much it looks like a bicycle, a penguin, a wig… Pick two of these scores. Every fish moves to a spot: how much it looks like the first (left → right) and the second (bottom → top).
